Fotógrafo: Susan Kae Grant

All images © Susan Kae Grant, courtesy of MW Editions, shared with permission Cryptic Photos by Susan kae Grant Plumb the World of Dreams in Night Journey' August 4, 2026 Books Photography Kate Mothes Share Pin Email Copy Bookmark A house spins out of control in a wind storm, animals loom large in the imaginations of children, and mysterious creatures lurk in the darkness of Susan kae Grant ’s shadow puppet-like photographs. For more than two decades, the artist has plumbed the mind's eye, especially what it sees when we sleep. In the early 1990s, she even began collaborating with a researcher in a sleep laboratory at UT Southwestern Medical Center. Study and documentation of what she visualized in her own REM cycle led to phrases and vocabulary about what she dreamt that she continues to tap into for her studio work. Forthcoming this autumn from MW Editions , Grant's book Night Journey delves into her lifelong fascination with the realm of the unconscious. Silhouetted figures and objects seemingly float across nearly 100 vignettes that are blurry, cryptic, and stretched around the edges as if they originate from somewhere we're unable to grasp. Through arresting black and white tableaux populated by fantastical creatures, animals, and children in hallucinatory settings, these photographs invite us to traverse the strangeness and chaos of breakneck contemporary life, like the dreams that inspired them, the publisher says. **Through Generations: Grandma Kinso & Granddaughter Yan-Han Preserve Native Language & Family Memories Through Storytelling**
Fotógrafos: shenj

Whenever our thoughts turn to the people we love, what stays with us are the little moments and small things that make them smile. But have you ever wondered how they see the world? Maybe the truest way to know someone isn’t through what they say, but through what they see. So we’re passing the camera around, seeing our loved ones through the moments that catch their eyes. Grandma Kinso and her granddaughter Yan-Han run the Instagram account Grandma Kinso's Stories, where Grandma shares stories from her nearly century-long life in the Taiwanese language, while Yan-Han brings these stories to life with her editing and subtitling. Through this approach, the two pass down memories and their mother tongue, as well as leave behind important records of multiple eras. We invited this grandmother-granddaughter duo to document their daily lives with the Lomo’Instant Automat and preserve the instant photos using the Soft Cover Photo Album. When photos take on a physical form and no longer remain as files on a phone, memories seem to carry more weight, and they even prompt Grandma Kinso to recall long-forgotten memories. Let’s step into her and Yan-Han's world, and listen to their stories that span multiple generations. © Grandma Kinso's Stories ,  Lomo’Instant Automat Hello, Grandma Kinso and Yan-Han. Welcome to the Magazine! Could you please introduce yourselves? Grandma Kinso: Hello, everyone. I’m Grandma Kinso. I was born in the fifth year of the Showa period (1930). I am 96 years old. I’m currently the oldest Taiwanese internet celebrity grandma! Since I was born in Taiwan under Japanese colonial rule, I speak Japanese and I love singing Japanese songs, I also enjoy chatting with people and sharing the fascinating stories of my life. Although I’ve had a difficult past, I’ve chosen to face life with optimism. Yan-Han: Hi, everyone! I’m Yan-Han, a “junior adult” 70 years younger than Grandma Kinso. In 2025, I unexpectedly found myself running “Grandma Kinso's Stories”. In addition to managing our social media and acting as Grandma’s agent, I work at an NGO full-time as an advocate and educator on social issues. © Grandma Kinso's Stories ,  Lomo’Instant Automat What kind of “unexpected” event led you to running “Grandma Kinso's Stories”? Yan-Han: When I was in college, I took a course on the history of Taiwan and interviewed my grandma. Later, in 2025, I posted the audio recording of that interview on Threads on a whim. In it, Grandma shared the story of how she was sold as a child, and to my surprise, it received a lot of attention and feedback. I decided to launch “Grandma Kinso's Stories” to begin carefully documenting Grandma’s unique life experiences while also gradually relearning my mother tongue – Taiwanese. I hope to save this language, which carries both culture and warmth, from the brink of extinction and ensure it is passed down from generation to generation. © Grandma Kinso's Stories ,  Lomo’Instant Automat How does Grandma feel about creating content and interacting with everyone online? Yan-Han: Grandma Kinso is both surprised and delighted! She thought people would naturally prefer to interact with young, good-looking influencers, so she wonders why so many people love listening to her talk. In her nineties, Grandma had grown bored with her monotonous daily life. However, since she started making videos to tell stories and sing, she’s found it a lot of joy! The encouraging comments and well-wishes from her fans have also made Grandma very happy. She’s gradually begun to see her own value and feel great about herself! © Grandma Kinso's Stories ,  Lomo’Instant Automat While running “Grandma Kinso’s Stories”, have you encountered any challenges or experiences that left a lasting impression on you? Grandma Kinso: What left the deepest impression on me was when a Japanese fan recorded a message for me. Since I went to school under Japanese rule, I was really happy that someone could chat with me in Japanese. Also, the way Japanese people speak sounds very different from how Taiwanese people speak. When I listen to them talk, their voices sound so soft! It’s really lovely! Yan-Han: I think the most memorable – and challenging – part has been creating content entirely in the Taiwanese language, including filming and producing videos for commercial partnerships! Because many promotional phrases or technical terms aren’t easy to express in Taiwanese; even a short line or two of voice-over actually takes us a lot of time and research to figure out the correct translation. Although Mandarin Chinese is a language I’m relatively more familiar with, I still hope to persist in using my mother tongue for my creative work. Aside from doing my part to revitalize our local language, it’s more like a social experiment: when content is produced entirely in Taiwanese, will the market or society still accept it? I want to challenge people to understand that the value of our native language isn’t limited to connecting with our elders; rather, like any other healthy language, it can be used in any situation and for any purpose – whether it’s composing poetry, shooting commercials, or buying 300 grams of pork belly at the market. So I’m also very grateful to everyone I’ve collaborated with, who have all been very understanding and supportive of my creative approach. © Grandma Kinso's Stories ,  Lomo’Instant Automat Does grandma have any notable memories or experiences related to film or photography? Grandma Kinso: Life was very hard back then and my family didn’t have any money, so we couldn’t afford a camera. I didn’t have the chance to have my photo taken when I was young. A memory that stands out the most took place in my 40s – a friend living in the U.S. told me he wanted a photo of me. So I went to a photo studio to have my picture taken, and I sent the photo to him. It’s the earliest photo of me I’ve been able to find so far. © Grandma Kinso's Stories Yan-Han, do you do photography in your day-to-day? Do you have any experience with film or instant cameras? Yan-Han: Taking photos with a smartphone counts as photography, right? So I guess I do it all the time! But I don’t get many chances to use a camera. I’ve used an instant camera before, too, but aside from this collaboration, I’ve mostly used one to take selfies with friends. Even though my face looks huge when I’m the one operating the camera, I kind of like that feeling of holding the camera with both hands, stretching my arms as far as I can, telling everyone to move closer to me, and counting, “One, two, three, cheese!”. It’s really fun! © Grandma Kinso's Stories ,  Lomo’Instant Automat How was your experience taking photos with the Lomo’Instant Automat? Grandma Kinso: It’s so cute and fun. Back in the day, you had to take the film to a photo lab to get the pictures developed, and you couldn’t let them get exposed to light! This is the first time I’ve seen a camera that prints photos right away. If you take a photo with someone, you can give them the print right away, and they’ll be so happy! A friend I met while hiking said she's going to show off a photo we took and tell people: “This is my mom!” Yan-Han: I usually use my phone to take videos and pictures of grandma, this was my first time taking photos of her with an instant camera! When using my phone, I’m used to taking lots of shots and then slowly picking out the best ones; but with instant photography, it’s very different. It feels like every shot I take is one less chance I have. So at first, I was a little nervous: “Is this okay? Should I use the flash? Should I adjust the focus?” – I kept asking myself these questions. After the “click”, and as the photo came out with a whir from the motor, my heart was filled with anticipation and excitement. Interestingly, even if the photos were out of focus or overexposed, they still had their own unique aesthetic and charm. So later on, I stopped being so cautious; I chose to enjoy the moment and happily accept the results. © Grandma Kinso's Stories What was it like for you to work on putting the album together? Grandma Kinso: It’s the kind of thing young people do. It’s fun to have someone to talk it over with; otherwise, I wouldn’t do any of this on my own! Taking photos and making an album like this was so much fun. These photos are very meaningful, and they bring back fond memories, so I’m really happy. Yan-Han: I found it incredibly therapeutic! These days, I’m so used to spending long hours on my electronic devices that it’s been a long time since I’ve taken the time to sit down and work with my hands. This time, I really tapped into my old passion for arts and crafts, dug out a bunch of ke-si (Taiwanese, “tools” or “props”), and in the process, I rediscovered a rare sense of peace and simple happiness. It also gave me more chances to chat with Grandma and look at photos together. I discovered that even though Grandma is getting older and her eyesight has declined a bit, she still has a keen artistic sense! She’d remind me from time to time: “Don’t make it too busy, leave some space,” or “Stick the photos at an angle, that looks more ‘artistic’.” It was awesome, grandma is the best. © Grandma Kinso's Stories ,  Lomo’Instant Automat Lastly, do you have any upcoming plans you’d like to share with us? Or is there anything else you’d like to say to our readers? Grandma Kinso: Thank you all so much for your encouragement and for watching my videos – it makes this old lady’s life happier and more carefree. Yan-Han: This year, we're working on a small project to publish a book in Taiwanese that tells the life story of Grandma Kinso. In addition to documenting Grandma’s fascinating (and perhaps star-crossed?) life, we also hope to preserve valuable corpus of the Taiwanese language. Feel free to follow us and stay tuned for updates. :) Huge thanks to Grandma Kinso and Yan-Han for sharing your photos and stories. We wish Grandma good health and all the best! Don’t forget to follow Grandma Kinso's Stories so you don’t miss any of Grandma’s wonderful tales and Taiwanese lessons!
