**&#8216;Bodega Cats of New York&#8217; Advocates for the City&#8217;s Beloved Feline Shopkeepers**
Fotógrafo: Gulce Kilkis and Dan Rimada

All images © Gulce Kilkis and Dan Rimada, courtesy of Epic Ink, shared with permission Bodega Cats of New York' Advocates for the City's Beloved Feline Shopkeepers August 5, 2026 Books Photography Kate Mothes Share Pin Email Copy Bookmark In 2022, New York resident and bonafide feline devotee Dan Rimada felt that something needed to be done to shine a light on the city's cats. The Big Apple not only has a real feral issue , but many of these creatures make their homes in iconic shops found around the city: bodegas. Over time, locals get to know them and they become fixtures of shelves and countertops. Technically, though, New York City considers it a health code violation to have animals in businesses that serve food. In spite of that, thousands of cats serve as mousers and rat catchers, and a lot of people don't realize that the snoozy tabby nestled atop the Doritos bags is actually illegal. Rimada founded Bodega Cats of New York to bring visibility to these beloved, informal shop staffers. This fall, a new book published by Epic Ink titled Bodega Cats of New York features 120 photos of 60 cats across all five boroughs along with their stories. The images are snapped by photographer Gulce Kilkis , who also runs the Instagram account @turkishstreetcats . Advocacy is as much a part of Rimada's mission as documenting kitties familiar to neighborhood customers, such as Oreo in Park Slope and Prince in SoHo. The type of care or attention the cats get can vary from store to store. Some are treated like royalty, while others are more like interlopers that are tolerated. Nevertheless, Rimada shares their individual stories and raises awareness of the wide range of realities for these animals. Although they are still in committee, two bills facilitated by Rimada have been introduced that would stop New York City agencies from treating a cat in a retail food store as an automatic New York City Health Code violation along with requiring voluntary, free vaccination and spay/neuter programs. The other bill would legally allow cats in retail stores as long as they met veterinary requirements. We want to protect what makes New York City feel like home, Rimada says in a statement. And that includes taking care of the cats that are a part of it. Bodega Cats of New York is slated for release in early November. Meet hundreds more feline friends on Instagram , and pre-order your copy on the project's website . You might also enjoy Marcel Heijnen's City Cats of Istanbul and a trip through art history with Phaidon's Cat .

**Defiance and pride in Chicano Camera Culture**

A Photographic History — A new major exhibition surveys Mexican American photography over six decades, platforming Chicano artists at a Chicano museum for the first time. Skyrocketing to fame in the late 1970s, comedian Cheech Marin parlayed his position as one half of beloved stoner duo Cheech &amp; Chong to become one of the world’s leading proponents of Chicano (Mexican American) art. In 2022, the Cheech Marin Center for Chicano Art &amp; Culture, aka The Cheech, opened its doors, making history as the first museum devoted exclusively to “all things Chicano art”.Hailing from Los Angeles, curator Elizabeth Ferrer remembers the museum’s opening as an important event, both for the community and for herself. Recognising she was among like minds, she set forth to create Chicano Camera Culture: A Photographic History, 1966-2026, an epic celebration of Chicano life as seen through 150 works across multiple generations now on view at the Cheech.Featuring nearly 50 photographers including trailblazers Louis Carlos Bernal, George Rodriguez, and María Varela; innovators Reynaldo Rivera, Kathy Vargas, and Ken Gonzales-Day; and contemporary artists Guadalupe Rosales, Star Montana, and Thalía Gochez, Chicano Camera Culture forms a map of photographic history that has been largely disregarded by industry and institutions alike. Read next: Down and out in east LA: vintage shots of Chicano life “The photographic record, whether from the viewpoint of photojournalists, documentarians, hobbyists, or artists, was created by the white gaze, and for a long time that fact was by and large taken for granted,” Ferrer says. “With the rise of the Civil Rights movement, the Chicano gaze comes into play. This means that for the first time, Chicanos are representing other Chicanos, typically, people who would otherwise be anonymous or unrecognised.” Like Ferrer’s 2021 book, Latinx Photography in the United States: A Visual History, Chicano Camera Culture is a landmark work, exploring Chicano photography as a cultural ethos born from knowledge of self. Rather than ascribe descriptors to identity, Ferrer maps its expression across generations, exploring the age-old questions of the human condition with a tender heart and a knowing eye. “All of the work in the show illuminates in one way or another, what it means to be Chicano,” she says. “Many of the themes addressed by the photographers – pride in identity, our struggle for agency and autonomy, the values we place in family and home, and the ways we create and claim space – are unsurprising. But what is impressive is the innovative ways in which they have been elaborated.” Read next: Vibrant photos of L.A.’s contemporary lowrider culture Organised around themes of self/portraiture, domestic life, protests, border stories, and alternate realities, Chicano Camera Culture is a magnificent archive of collective resistance against an occupying force that has vilified and criminalised indigenous and mestizo communities, while telling the stories of the people and their cultures.“From the beginning, the Chicano gaze has been about expressing pride in Chicano identity, preserving our history, uplifting cultural and spiritual values, validating persons out of the mainstream, and giving visibility to members of our own community,” Ferrer says. “As for what it means to tell Chicano stories by Chicano artists in a Chicano museum in the United States in 2026, I’d say it’s high time!” Chicano Camera Culture: A Photographic History, 1966–2026 is on view through September 6, 2026, at the Cheech Marin Art Center in Riverside, California.
